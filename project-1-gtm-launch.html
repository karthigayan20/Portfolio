Launching an AI module into a partner-led channel.

LumaLocal sells local marketing software to US small businesses, mostly through agency partners who resell it under their own brand. This is the complete go-to-market package for a new review-response module — positioning, ICP, competitive set, pricing, messaging, enablement and a 13-week scorecard measured against targets set before launch.

Business problem
A new AI module was built with no plan for how partners would sell it. Previous module launches attached to under 20% of accounts.
Objective
55.0% attach rate on active accounts and 275 partner reps enabled within 90 days, at $50 incremental MRR per account.
My role
Positioning and messaging, competitive research, pricing input, enablement assets, launch plan, and the measurement framework.
Result
Attach rate and enablement beat target; closed-won and revenue came in under it because demo volume ramped late.

Positioning

One statement everything else derives from

For US local businesses and the agencies that manage them, who lose customers to reviews that sit unanswered for days, Reputation AI is a review-response layer inside the marketing platform they already log into — it drafts on-brand replies in minutes and reports sentiment by location. Unlike standalone reputation tools, it needs no new login, no new contract, and it bills through the partner.

Category we compete in

Not "AI writing". We compete for the reputation-management line item an agency already sells, or the hour an owner already spends.

Primary value

Response time and consistency — the two things that move review ratings and that manual replying always fails at.

Proof we lead with

Median response time and reply coverage per location, shown in the partner's own monthly client report.

Who we sell to

Three segments, deliberately ranked
Priority 1 · channel

The agency partner

Profile
5–50 SMB clients, 2–10 staff, sells retainers of $500–$2,500 a month.
Pain
Review management is manual, inconsistent, and mostly unbilled.
Buying trigger
A client asks why their rating dropped.
What wins
A billable line item they can white-label and defend in a report.
Priority 2 · expansion

Multi-location operator

Profile
3–20 locations: dental groups, home services, restaurant groups.
Pain
No consistent voice across locations and no comparable view of sentiment.
Buying trigger
One location dragging the brand average down.
What wins
Location-level comparison and approval workflow before replies post.
Priority 3 · volume

Single-location owner

Profile
Owner-operator, no marketing staff, self-serve signup.
Pain
Replies late or not at all; every reply is written from scratch.
Buying trigger
A bad review they do not know how to answer.
What wins
A draft waiting in the inbox. Price sensitivity is high here.

Ranking matters more than the descriptions: agencies get the enablement budget because one partner carries dozens of accounts.

Pricing & packaging

Built for a reseller margin
Retail price$39 per location, per month
Partner wholesale$19 51% partner margin
Target ARPA uplift$50 on $149 average account
Trial30 days auto-enabled, opt-out

Why this structure

  • Per-location pricing lets the multi-location segment grow the account without a renegotiation.
  • A 51% partner margin is the number partners need to justify selling it themselves rather than absorbing it into an existing retainer.
  • Auto-enabled trial removes the partner's decision to introduce it — the attach-rate lever that mattered most in the results below.

Objection handling

  • "AI replies will sound robotic." Approval workflow on by default; the reply is a draft until a human posts it.
  • "My clients won't pay more." Sell against staff time, and show the per-location coverage report in the next client review.
  • "We already reply to reviews." Median response time is the wedge — nearly every account replies to some reviews, and almost none reply to all of them.

Launch plan

Four phases · what shipped in each
T−6 to T−3 weeks
Readiness

Get the story straight before anyone sells it

Positioning statement signed off by product and sales. Pricing modelled against partner margin. Competitive archetypes documented. Success metrics and targets agreed in writing — before launch, so nobody could redefine success afterwards.

T−2 weeks
Partner beta

12 partners, 40 accounts, one week of feedback

Beta with the partners most likely to complain loudly. Two changes came out of it: approval workflow moved on by default, and the client-facing report was rebuilt around response time rather than review volume.

Retrospective

Including the part that did not work

What worked

  • Trial on by default. It removed the partner's decision to introduce the module and is the single biggest reason attach beat target.
  • Enablement shipped before the announcement, so partner reps were never the bottleneck.
  • Targets agreed in writing pre-launch, which made the shortfall diagnosable instead of arguable.

What did not

  • Demo volume finished 14% under target. Partners were enabled but had nothing to run a campaign with until week 5.
  • Revenue followed demand, not readiness: $8,562 against a $9,500 target.
  • Priority 3 pricing was never tested. The single-location segment is the most price sensitive and got the least attention.

What I would change

  • Treat the partner co-marketing kit as a launch blocker, not a phase-two asset.
  • Add a demand-side target — booked demos per enabled rep — so the scorecard cannot look healthy while pipeline is flat.
  • Run a pricing test on the single-location segment in the beta window.
